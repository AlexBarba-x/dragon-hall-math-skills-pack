Lesson 1 of 4

Signed mixed numbers

You will add a negative mixed number to a positive mixed number when the parts have different bottom numbers.

Goal

Convert each mixed number. Glue every sign to its own number. Rewrite the parts in one common unit. Then add. You should be able to say why the sign travels with the number.

Commons lessons cited: DH-MATH-5-NF-A-1-mixed-L001 (Wholes and Parts Together) and DH-MATH-7-NS-A-1d-L001 (Rearranging a Sum on Purpose). Concept checks: DH-MATH-5-NF-A-1-mixed-CC, DH-MATH-7-NS-A-1d-CC.

Concrete · gather wholes, recut parts

Say Put two mixed numbers on the mat in your mind. Gather wholes with wholes. The parts only add after they are the same size. If a half meets five eighths, trade the half for four eighths first.

Do On paper, draw two rows: a wholes row and a parts row. For \(2\frac{1}{2} + 1\frac{5}{8}\), write wholes \(2 + 1\) and parts \(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{5}{8}\).

Listen for Four eighths and five eighths make nine eighths. Eight of those snap into one more whole, with one eighth left. The sum is \(4\frac{1}{8}\).

Pictorial · the sign is glued on

Say Now one mixed number is negative. The two addition laws talk about adding only. Rewrite first so every sign rides with its number. A minus in front of a mixed number belongs to the whole value, not just the whole-number part.

Do Rewrite \(-2\frac{1}{3} + 1\frac{5}{6}\) as adding a negative mixed number to a positive one. Turn each mixed number into one signed fraction.

Listen for \(-2\frac{1}{3} = -\dfrac{7}{3}\) and \(1\frac{5}{6} = \dfrac{11}{6}\). The minus stays in front of seven thirds. It does not get left behind.

If stuck Ask: which number does that minus belong to? Then insist on the rewrite as the first line.

Abstract · common unit, then signed sum

Say Thirds and sixths are different sizes. Recut both fractions into sixths, then add the signed parts. Scan before you grind: after the rewrite, the chain is a pure sum.

Do Convert \(-\dfrac{7}{3}\) to sixths and add \(\dfrac{11}{6}\).

Listen for \(-\dfrac{14}{6} + \dfrac{11}{6} = -\dfrac{3}{6} = -\dfrac{1}{2}\).

Worked example

Compute \(-2\frac{1}{3} + 1\frac{5}{6}\).

  1. Rewrite as signed impropers: \(-\dfrac{7}{3} + \dfrac{11}{6}\).
  2. Common unit sixths: \(-\dfrac{14}{6} + \dfrac{11}{6}\).
  3. Add the numerators: \(-14 + 11 = -3\), so \(-\dfrac{3}{6}\).
  4. Simplify: \(-\dfrac{1}{2}\).

The rows way agrees: wholes \(-2 + 1 = -1\), parts \(-\dfrac{2}{6} + \dfrac{5}{6} = \dfrac{3}{6}\), then \(-1 + \dfrac{1}{2} = -\dfrac{1}{2}\).

Interactive check

Write your answer, then check. A grown-up opens the reveal only if you need it.

1. Compute \(-3\frac{1}{4} + 2\frac{5}{8}\). Give a simplified fraction.

2. Compute \(-1\frac{2}{5} + 2\frac{1}{2}\). Mixed number or improper fraction is fine.

Adult only — reveal keys

1. \(-\dfrac{13}{4}+\dfrac{21}{8}=-\dfrac{26}{8}+\dfrac{21}{8}=-\dfrac{5}{8}\).

2. \(-\dfrac{7}{5}+\dfrac{5}{2}=-\dfrac{14}{10}+\dfrac{25}{10}=\dfrac{11}{10}=1\frac{1}{10}\).

Watch for adding unlike parts without converting, or dropping the sign when the number is rewritten.
